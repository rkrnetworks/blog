How do we bring all the cooks onto the same page?

A data center build-out involves a lot of people. Designers, network engineers, data center technicians, electricians, mechanical contractors, and whoever ends up running the hall afterwards. Each group works from its own drawings, its own lists and its own idea of what's already in place. This post is about DC automation and operations in RKR SETU 1.0, and it's for the people who make a build-out possible.

## How a build-out usually goes

Most build-outs run on a pile of documents. A floor plan here, a rack elevation there, a cable schedule in a spreadsheet and a bill of materials in another. They start out consistent. Then the work begins, things move, parts get substituted, and the documents drift apart.

The result is familiar to anyone who has worked a site. The tray isn't where the drawing says. The rack count doesn't match the order. Someone asks whether the grounding is done in row three, and the honest answer is that someone would need to go and look.

It starts as a grid of floor pedestals, 600 millimetres apart. Then the raised floor, the overhead supports and the cable trays go in, piece by piece.

Every part carries its own bill of materials ID, and the count keeps climbing as the hall takes shape. The racks go in row by row. Then the pipework, and the units that feed power and cooling. Doors, aisles, fibre and grounding follow, all in the same model.

By the end, there are 2,097 items and 10,504 parts, in one synthetic twin.

## Why one model matters for a build

The value isn't the picture. It's that everyone is looking at the same thing. When the electrician, the network engineer and the technician all work from one model, the question of what's in row three has one answer, and it's the same answer for everyone.

It also means the model carries on after the build. The hall that was planned and built in the twin is the same hall that gets run and managed afterwards. The next post in this series switches the hall on, and things start to break the way they do on any live floor.

This fits how we think about the company as a whole. We build synthetic twins for all: switching, routing, wireless, firewalls and security, data centers, power and cooling, and compute. A build-out touches nearly every one of those, which is why it needs one model rather than six.

## Practical takeaways

- Give every part an identity from the start. A part with an ID can be tracked, counted and checked. A part on a drawing can only be looked for.
- Keep one model for the whole team rather than one document per trade.
- Build the hall in the order it's built on site: floor, overhead, racks, then power, cooling and cabling. It makes gaps easier to see.
- Plan for the model to outlive the build. The people who run the hall will need it as much as the people who built it.
