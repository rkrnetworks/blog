RKR SETU 1.0 is generally available in India, APAC and EMEA. We announced it with a short video, and rather than fill it with slides, we used it to look a little further ahead: a humanoid fitting an outdoor access point, six metres up the north wall of a building.

This post covers what the launch means and what the video shows.

## What generally available means here

SETU 1.0 is the synthetic twin we've been building for network, IT and data center operations. Generally available means teams in India, APAC and EMEA can now work with us on it. Documentation has already been shared with customers, and it can be obtained on request through our team. North America followed later, and we cover that in a separate post.

We'd rather show outcomes than talk about them, so the rest of this post is about the install.

## Why an outdoor access point

Outdoor installs are a good example of field work where small details decide the result. The mount has to be right, the bond to ground has to come before anything powered is touched, and the cable has to come in so that water can't follow it into the seal. Get any of those wrong and the access point may work on day one and fail after the first heavy rain.

Those steps are usually checked by eye, by one person, at height. A missed step isn't noticed until something goes wrong much later.

## The install, step by step

First, the mount. It goes on the column face, with four fixings. Then the ground bond, before anything powered is touched. Next, the access point itself is lifted into place. It hangs from the bracket and is secured with two screws.

Each step is checked off on the panel before the next one begins. That's the part we want people to notice. The work isn't one long task, it's a list of steps, and each one has a check.

Then the cable and the gland. The cable comes in from the side, with a drip loop 150 millimetres below the entry, so water runs off before it reaches the seal.

One check fails. The gland nut is loose. It's tightened, and the check passes. The drip loop is tied, and power over Ethernet comes on. Then a link check with a handheld: a strong signal on channel 157, and every step on the list is green.

## Why we think this is worth showing

We believe this kind of field work is something humanoids could handle within this decade. Whether that happens on that timeline or not, the idea behind it matters today. When every step is written down and checked against a model of the site, a loose gland nut gets caught on the wall, not months later in a fault ticket.

The same applies to people doing the work. A clear step list, a check after each step, and a record of what passed are useful whoever holds the spanner.

## Practical takeaways

- Write outdoor installs as a step list with a check for each step, not as one task.
- Bond to ground before anything powered is touched.
- Bring cables in from the side or below, with a drip loop under the entry.
- Finish with a link check on site, not only a light on the access point.
- Keep the record. What passed, and when, is the starting point for any later fault.
