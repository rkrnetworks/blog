The plan sounds simple: replace the old access points one for one with new ones that support 6 GHz, mount them in the same places, and give modern devices a fast, uncrowded band. Mounting brackets match, cabling is already there, and the change can happen floor by floor.

Then the first floor goes in, and users near the edges report that their new laptops prefer the older band, or switch between bands more than they used to. The predictive 6 GHz coverage map, built from the old floor plan, showed green everywhere. The building had changed since then.

## Same spots, different reach

Higher frequencies lose more energy over distance and through materials. At 6 GHz, signal fades faster than at 5 GHz, and walls, glass and people absorb more of it. An access point placement that gave solid 5 GHz coverage may leave gaps at 6 GHz, especially behind concrete cores, in rooms with coated glass and in corners furthest from the ceiling mounts.

On top of that, 6 GHz client devices often transmit at lower power than on other bands. The access point may reach the client, but the client's reply is weaker. That imbalance shows up as poor performance at the edges even where the bars look acceptable.

And not every device supports the new band. During a long transition, the network is serving two populations, and the old bands still need to be designed properly too.

## Why the one-for-one swap is attractive

It is tempting because it is light on effort and disruption. No new cable runs, no new ceiling work, no new survey. The risk is that it treats the access point locations as fixed, when they were chosen for a different band, often years ago, in a building that has since been refitted.

## Comparing bands on a made-up floor

Take an illustrative office floor with a central concrete core, glass-walled meeting rooms along one side and an open plan area along the other. The synthetic twin holds the walls, glass, core and the current access point positions.

At 5 GHz, the twin shows coverage across the floor, with a little thinning behind the core. With the same positions at 6 GHz, three areas stand out: the strip behind the core, the meeting rooms with coated glass, and the far corner of the open plan area. In those spots, a typical laptop would sit at the edge of usable 6 GHz service and would likely fall back to 5 GHz.

The twin then makes it easy to test small changes. Moving two access points a few meters and adding one more behind the core closes most of the gaps. Leaving the positions alone but planning the meeting rooms as 5 GHz areas is also an option. Both can be compared against the old design, with each band shown separately.

## Planning a 6 GHz upgrade

- Do not assume the old access point positions are right for the new band.
- Check coverage behind cores, stairwells and coated glass separately for 6 GHz.
- Account for client devices transmitting at lower power in the new band.
- Keep designing the older bands properly for devices that cannot use 6 GHz.
- Pilot one floor and look at which band clients actually use at the edges.
- Expect a small number of moves or additions, and plan for them up front.

The new band is worth having. It just needs to be planned as a new band, not as a firmware option on the old layout.
